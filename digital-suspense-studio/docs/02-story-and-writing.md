# Story and Writing Guide

## Core concept

Every video is a fictional suspense story experienced through a digital device.

The phone interface is the stage. The viewer understands the story by reading
messages and watching notifications, calls, and other interface events.

## Tone

- Tense, mysterious, and plausible within the story's fictional world.
- Concise and conversational.
- Suggestive rather than graphic.
- Avoid excessive exposition, clichés, and unnecessary jargon.

## Narrative structure

### 1. Hook

The hook is the first 3 seconds and the first spoken line together. It has to do
three jobs at once: state a situation the viewer understands instantly, contain
something that cannot be true, and leave one question open.

Arrive late. Start mid-event, not at the beginning of the evening: no waking up,
no commuting, no establishing shot. Withhold the explanation on purpose — context
is the reward for watching, not the price of admission.

The three cold-open shapes that work in a vertical feed:
- **Impossible image** — something that should not exist, staged plainly.
- **Mid-crisis** — open inside the worst second of an event already underway.
- **Aftermath** — open on the result, and make the reel the explanation.

Say the impossible thing out loud in the first line, in the character's own voice,
in the present tense. Twelve words beats thirty: *"I'm holding my phone. And it
says I'm two point eight kilometres away."* The line states the situation, the
impossibility, and the question, and it takes three seconds.

Do not open with an explanation of the anomaly. Do not open with a logo, a title,
or a caption the viewer has to read before anything happens.

The hook must be readable long enough to register — and every hook needs a turn:
by second eight, a detail that makes the question bigger instead of answering it.

### 2. Setup

Clarify who is involved and why the event is strange.

### 3. Escalation

Each new message or interface event should change what the viewer understands.

Use two or three escalating developments rather than a long conversation.

### 4. Twist

Reveal a surprising detail that follows from information already shown.

### 5. Ending

Provide a short payoff, unsettling final line, or purposeful cliffhanger.

The ending should feel like an ending, not merely stop mid-sentence.

## Voice-over

**Every reel is narrated.** The pictures show what the phone is doing; the voice
says what it costs. Neither carries the story alone.

- Write the voice-over first, as speech, in the character's own words. If the
  lines do not work read aloud, the story does not work.
- First person, present tense. The narrator is inside the event, not reporting it.
- One idea per line. 6–14 words. A 60 s reel carries 10–14 lines, a 30 s reel 5–7.
- The voice may state facts the screen cannot show (who she is, what she is
  walking home from, who is waiting). It may never explain the anomaly.
- Leave room to breathe: silence is a line too. Cut the voice off on the twist
  rather than talking over it.
- Captions are a second layer and never a transcript of these lines.
## Writing for a phone screen

- Prefer short messages and simple sentences.
- Keep names, timestamps, and interface labels easy to distinguish.
- Show only what is needed to understand the story.
- Avoid adding new characters unless they are necessary.
- Hold important text long enough to read.
- Do not rely on tiny background text for the plot.
- Keep the story understandable with sound off.

As a starting guideline, allow roughly one second per 2–3 short words, plus
time for the viewer to orient themselves. Adjust after reviewing the preview.

## Fiction disclosure

Show a clear, readable "FICTIONAL STORY" label at the opening or ending.
The label must remain visible long enough to read.

The publishing caption must also state that the story is fictional.

Do not portray fictional screenshots as evidence of actual events.

## Safety and identity

- Use invented names, accounts, locations, and phone numbers.
- Avoid real private individuals, identifying information, and impersonation.
- Avoid graphic violence, explicit sexual content, and instructions for harm.
- Do not frame harassment, stalking, or self-harm as something to imitate.
- Do not use real emergency alerts in a way likely to mislead viewers.

## Example beat sequence

Topic: A woman receives a voicemail from tomorrow.

1. Hook: A voicemail arrives, dated tomorrow.
2. Setup: The caller is listed as "You".
3. Escalation: The message says not to answer the next call.
4. Escalation: Her phone begins ringing.
5. Twist: The incoming caller is also "You".
6. Ending: The voicemail transcript adds: "I didn't listen either."

This is a structural example, not a required script.
